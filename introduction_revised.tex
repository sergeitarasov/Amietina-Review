

\section*{NEW Introduction}

Taxonomic revisions turn observations into species descriptions, diagnoses, identification keys, and nomenclatural acts. Yet their preparation is still largely document-based: observations are rewritten as prose, edited in a manuscript, and reformatted for publication, typically in Microsoft Word or Google Docs. Repeatedly moving information between data and text takes time, makes it harder to keep them consistent, and can introduce errors. It also leaves much of the information in taxonomic publications difficult to find and reuse \citep{smith2013beyond, miller2012cybertaxonomic, penev2017strategies}.

This problem is especially clear in species descriptions. Natural language is easy for taxonomists to read, but computers cannot reliably identify the anatomical characters and their relationships described in the text. These observations must therefore be extracted and recoded before they can be searched, compared across taxa, or combined with other data. Semantic descriptions based on ontologies address this limitation by representing observations with standardized terms and explicit relationships \citep{balhoff2013semantic, deans2015phenotypes}. Phenoscript provides a formal language for writing such descriptions and can generate both machine-readable data and human-readable text for a conventional taxonomic treatment \citep{montanaro2024beyond, montanaro2024computable}.

Generating readable text, however, does not solve the problem if it must be copied into the manuscript again after every change. We therefore connect Phenoscript, GitHub, and Overleaf in a single workflow to streamline this process. Phenoscript is the source of the species descriptions, Git records changes to the descriptions and related files, and Overleaf compiles the Latex manuscript containing the generated text. Corrections made in Phenoscript can be carried into the manuscript without copying and pasting, while the history of the project remains available on GitHub. This approach keeps the manuscript closer to its source data and makes collaboration more transparent and reproducible \citep{ram2013git, braga2023github, himmelstein2020open}. Because \LaTeX{} separates content from formatting, the same source can also be adapted to journal templates with less manual reformatting \citep{bar2021reproducible, krewinkel2017formatting}.

Here, we use this workflow in a taxonomic review of the Afrotropical dung beetle genus \textit{Amietina} Cambefort, 1981 (Coleoptera: Scarabaeinae: Onthophagini). The genus currently contains four described species from forests in West, Central, and eastern Africa \citep{cambefort1981amietina, branco1989deux}. Material collected in the Eastern Arc Mountains of Tanzania revealed four additional species, broadening the morphological limits of the genus. The distributions of these species also add to the limited knowledge of dung beetle endemism across the Eastern Arc mountain blocks \citep{burgess2007biological, montanaro2024microallopatric}.

We describe \textit{A. bondarevi} \textbf{n. sp.}, \textit{A. grebennikovi} \textbf{n. sp.}, \textit{A. mazumbaiensis} \textbf{n. sp.}, and \textit{A. praedicta} \textbf{n. sp.}; describe the previously unknown female of \textit{A. nyassalandica}; provide new distributional records; and present an updated identification key. Alongside these taxonomic contributions, we show how Phenoscript, GitHub, and Overleaf can connect semantic phenotype data directly to a publication-ready manuscript.

Taxonomic studies depend on the accurate production, management, and publication of large quantities of descriptive information. Most taxonomic manuscripts are prepared using general-purpose word processors, particularly Microsoft Word, including its cloud-based version, or Google Docs. These applications provide familiar writing environments and support comments, suggested edits, and real-time collaboration. Nevertheless, they have important limitations for taxonomic projects, which are often data-intensive and involve repeated revision of structured descriptive content \citep{smith2013beyond, penev2017strategies}. Changes may become scattered across multiple document versions, making revisions difficult to track and earlier formulations hard to recover, especially in collaborative projects. Considerable effort may also be required to adapt a manuscript to journal-specific formatting requirements, diverting time from scientific analysis  to largely mechanical editorial tasks.

Another critical limitation is that conventional taxonomic descriptions are written primarily as natural-language text. Although they communicate morphological information effectively to human readers, their contents are not directly interpretable by computers. This constrains the automated extraction, integration, and comparison of phenotypic information across publications and taxa, as well as its reuse in broader biodiversity studies. Phenoscript addresses this problem by using biological ontologies to encode traits semantically \citep{ADD_PHENOSCRIPT_REFERENCE}. Its descriptions can be rendered as both human-readable text for publication and machine-readable data for computational querying and integration. In these human-readable descriptions, phenotypic terms are hyperlinked to online ontology services, allowing readers to access standardized definitions directly from the text. This reduces ambiguity, promotes consistency of terminology, and facilitates comparisons across descriptions and studies.

In a basic Phenoscript-assisted workflow, the generated natural-language description can be copied into the manuscript. However, this retains an important source of duplication: whenever the original Phenoscript description is corrected or expanded, the text must be regenerated and copied again. Together with the broader challenges of manuscript preparation, including version control and journal-specific formatting, this repeated transfer requires additional time, creates opportunities for error, and increases the risk of divergence between the underlying observations and the final publication.


Here, we present an integrated workflow for streamlining the production of semantic taxonomic manuscripts by reducing repetitive copying, providing transparent version control, and minimizing time spent on formatting. Phenoscript descriptions are written in \href{https://code.visualstudio.com/}{Visual Studio Code}, a freely available text editor that supports the Phenoscript language. The complete taxonomic project is stored in \href{https://github.com/}{GitHub}, an online platform that records changes to files, allows collaborators to compare revisions and recover earlier versions, and can provide public access to all materials associated with the study. The manuscript is prepared in \href{https://www.overleaf.com/}{Overleaf}, a collaborative online platform for writing LaTeX documents that supports simultaneous editing, automatic compilation, reference management, and journal-specific templates \citep{sanchez_egea_garcia_vilana_gonzalez_rojas_napoles_alberro_2026}. GitHub connects the Phenoscript files with Overleaf, allowing updated human-readable descriptions to be synchronized with the manuscript without repeated copying and pasting. Together, these tools provide taxonomists with a reproducible workflow in which descriptions are written once, their revision history is preserved, and publication-ready text is generated and incorporated directly into a submission-ready  manuscript. By storing openly accessible files in GitHub and encoding phenotypic information with standardized ontology terms, the workflow also makes taxonomic data more findable, accessible, interoperable, and reusable in accordance with the FAIR principles.

\section*{Introduction}
%---
Taxonomic revisions depend on the accurate production, management, and publication of large amounts of descriptive information. Traditionally, morphological observations are first recorded in notes, spreadsheets, or specialized databases and are then manually rewritten or copied into species descriptions for publication. This separation between data collection and manuscript preparation creates duplicated work and increases the risk of inconsistencies, transcription errors, and divergence between the underlying observations and the final published text. Moreover, conventional taxonomic descriptions are written primarily for human readers, which limits their direct use in computational analyses, database integration, and large-scale comparisons across taxa.

%----------
In recent years, digital tools and data infrastructures have become increasingly important for modern taxonomy, enabling more transparent, reproducible, and interoperable research \citep{penev2017strategies, smith2013cybertaxonomy}. Semantic description languages, collaborative writing platforms, code repositories, and standardized biodiversity data formats address different aspects of the taxonomic workflow, but are often used independently. Integrating these components into a unified framework can streamline taxonomic practice while improving the accessibility of taxonomic knowledge.

We developed an integrated taxonomic workflow that combines semantic morphological descriptions written in Phenoscript, version-controlled data management through GitHub, and collaborative manuscript preparation in Overleaf. Phenoscript is a semantic language that encodes phenotypic information in both human- and machine-readable formats, enabling morphological descriptions to be interpreted by taxonomists while being computationally reusable \citep{montanaro2024beyond, montanaro2024computable}. Overleaf is a LaTex-based collaborative writing platform that supports real-time editing, version tracking, and the integration of structured data into scientific manuscripts \citep{sanchez_egea_garcia_vilana_gonzalez_rojas_napoles_alberro_2026}. GitHub serves as the central repository linking Phenoscript and Overleaf, providing version control, collaborative editing, and public access to all files associated with this publication. This integration ensures that updates to the source files are automatically reflected in the manuscript while minimizing transcription errors \citep{dabbish2012social}.

To further enhance interoperability, nomenclatural acts are published as nanopublications \citep{kuhn2017genuine, kuhn2013broadening, rossini2024towards}, machine-readable units of knowledge that can be readily discovered and reused on the Semantic Web, while distributional data are released in Darwin Core format for integration with biodiversity repositories \citep{wieczorek2012darwin}. Together, these approaches follow FAIR principles by improving the findability, accessibility, interoperability, and reusability of taxonomic data \citep{wilkinson2016fair}.

Here, we implement this workflow by reviewing the dung beetle genus \textit{Amietina} Cambefort, 1981 (Coleoptera: Scarabaeidae: Onthophagini). \textit{Amietina} belongs to the hyperdiverse tribe Onthophagini (\textit{sensu} \citealp{Montanaro2026.07.22.740134}) and is one of the few genera presenting only 8 antennomeres instead of the usual 9 \citep{branco1992essai, cambefort1981amietina, cambefort1979donnees, cambefort1986une, deschodt2023new}. The genus is well characterised by a unique combination of homoplastic traits: 1) the absence of a 4\textsuperscript{th} tooth on the protibial margin; 2) the distal protibial margin most often perpendicularly truncated, always so in males; 3) the presence of an uncus in the distoventral margin of male protibiae; 4) the bidentate clypeus; 5) the absence of any head protrusion except for a blunt, transverse gibbosity; and 6) the body small and lenticular \citep{branco1989deux, cambefort1981amietina}. \textit{Amietina} currently comprises four described species, two of them found in West and Central-West African forests (\textit{A. larrochei} Cambefort, 1981 and \textit{A. eburnea} Cambefort, 1981), one in Uganda (\textit{A. cambeforti} Branco, 1988) and one in Malawi (\textit{A. nyassalandica} Branco, 1988) \citep{branco1989deux, cambefort1981amietina}.

During an ongoing survey of the dung beetle fauna of the Tanzanian Eastern Arc Mountains biodiversity hotspot undertaken by GM, we gathered material of four very similar, undescribed species of Onthophagini from the rainforests of the Udzungwa, Uluguru and West Usambara Mountains. The taxa match \textit{Amietina} for most traits, yet they differ in having the protibiae always obliquely truncated and a carina or tubercle on the head. In which onthophagine (sub)genus should these species be included? To understand it, we studied the morphology of all described \textit{Amietina}, including phylogenetically informative characters of male genitalia that were never employed before. This leads us to expand and redefine the concept of the genus, in which we accommodate the four new taxa from Tanzania---\textit{Amietina praedicta} n. sp., \textit{Amietina grebennikovi} n. sp., \textit{Amietina bondarevi} n. sp. and \textit{Amietina mazumbaiensis} n. sp. Moreover, we describe for the first time the female of \textit{A. nyassalandica}, provide new distributional records and an updated identification key to the genus. Lastly, we stress how the new taxa represent the first documented case of dung beetle microendemism in the Eastern Arc mountains in which closely allied species diversified allopatrically in different mountains.

The same structure may also be named differently by different authors, while the meaning of a term may depend on the taxonomic group or context. These observations must therefore be extracted and recoded before they can be searched, compared across taxa, or combined with other data.

Semantic descriptions based on ontologies address this limitation by representing observations with standardized terms and explicit relationships \citep{balhoff2013semantic, deans2015phenotypes}. In simple terms, an ontology provides agreed names and definitions for biological structures and specifies how those structures are related. For example, it can distinguish an anatomical structure from one of its qualities and record that the structure is part of a particular organism. Each term has a stable identifier, allowing descriptions that use the same concept to be linked even when their wording differs. Phenoscript provides a formal language for writing such descriptions and can generate both machine-readable data and human-readable text for a conventional taxonomic treatment \citep{montanaro2024beyond, montanaro2024computable}. Taxonomists can therefore read the resulting description in a familiar form, while its underlying content remains available for computational searches and comparisons.

The descriptions are written once in Phenoscript and rendered directly in the manuscript. When a description is corrected, its output can be regenerated and synchronized through GitHub instead of being copied, reformatted, and checked again by hand. This reduces the risk that the published treatment differs from its semantic source.

The same connection also makes the wider manuscript easier to manage. GitHub keeps the descriptions, manuscript, bibliography, figures, and processing files together and preserves a history of their changes. Collaborators can see what was changed, compare versions, and recover earlier content. Overleaf provides a shared writing environment and automatically compiles these files into the formatted paper. This approach keeps the manuscript closer to its source data and makes collaboration more transparent and reproducible \citep{ram2013git, braga2023github, himmelstein2020open}.
