\begin{abstract}
Taxonomic studies generate large amount of descriptive information, yet species treatments are usually written directly as natural-language text. This approach makes the underlying phenotypic information difficult to extract, reuse computationally, and maintain consistently across studies. 
We present an integrated workflow connecting
\href{https://marketplace.visualstudio.com/items?itemName=Tarasov-Lab.phenoscript}{Phenoscript},
\href{https://github.com/}{GitHub}, and
\href{https://www.overleaf.com/}{Overleaf}.
Species descriptions are written semantically in Phenoscript using biological ontologies, making their phenotypic content computable and reusable; they are then stored and version-controlled in GitHub and synchronized with an Overleaf manuscript, where human-readable descriptions are rendered directly for publication. The workflow reduces repetitive copying and formatting, preserves revision history, and promotes the production of FAIR phenotypic and taxonomic data.

We apply this workflow to the African dung beetle genus \textit{Amietina} Cambefort, 1981 (Coleoptera: Scarabaeinae: Onthophagini), and describe four new species from Tanzania---\textit{A. bondarevi} \textbf{n. sp.}, \textit{A. grebennikovi} \textbf{n. sp.}, \textit{A. mazumbaiensis} \textbf{n. sp.}, and \textit{A. praedicta} \textbf{n. sp.}---bringing the total number of recognized species  to eight. We also provide new distributional records, present an updated identification key, and discuss microendemism in \textit{Amietina} across the Eastern Arc Mountains.

\textbf{Keywords:} LaTex, collaborative writing, FAIR principles, new species, Onthophagini, Eastern Arc Mountains, Tanzania

\end{abstract}

\section*{Introduction}

Taxonomic revisions turn observations into species descriptions, diagnoses, identification keys, and nomenclatural acts. Yet their preparation is still largely document-based: observations are rewritten as prose, edited in a manuscript, and reformatted for publication, typically in Microsoft Word or Google Docs. Repeatedly moving information between data and text takes time, makes it harder to keep them consistent, and can introduce errors. It also leaves much of the information in taxonomic publications difficult to find and reuse \citep{smith2013beyond, miller2012cybertaxonomic, penev2017strategies}.

This problem is especially clear in species descriptions. Natural language is easy for taxonomists to read, but computers cannot reliably identify the anatomical characters and their relationships described in the text. The same structure may also be named differently by different authors, while the meaning of a term may depend on the taxonomic group or context. These observations must therefore be extracted and recoded before they can be searched, compared across taxa, or combined with other data.

Semantic descriptions based on ontologies address this limitation by representing observations with standardized terms and explicit relationships \citep{balhoff2013semantic, deans2015phenotypes}. In simple terms, an ontology provides agreed names and definitions for biological terms and specifies how they are related. Each term has a stable identifier, allowing descriptions that use the same concept to be linked even when their wording differs. Phenoscript provides a formal language for writing such descriptions and can generate both machine-readable data and human-readable text for a conventional taxonomic treatment \citep{montanaro2024beyond, montanaro2024computable}. Taxonomists can therefore read the resulting description in a familiar form, while its underlying content remains available for computational searches and comparisons.

Generating readable text, however, does not solve the problem if it must be copied into the manuscript again after every change. We therefore connect Phenoscript, GitHub, and Overleaf in a single workflow to streamline this process. Phenoscript is the source of the species descriptions, Git records changes to the descriptions and related files, and Overleaf compiles the \LaTeX{} manuscript containing the generated text. The descriptions are written once in Phenoscript and rendered directly in the manuscript. When a description is corrected, its output can be regenerated and synchronized through GitHub instead of being copied, reformatted, and checked again by hand. This streamlines the manuscript preparation and reduces the risk that the published treatment differs from its semantic source.

The same connection also makes the wider manuscript easier to manage. GitHub keeps the descriptions, manuscript, bibliography, figures, and processing files together and preserves a history of their changes. Collaborators can see what was changed, compare versions, and recover earlier content. Overleaf provides a shared writing environment and automatically compiles these files into the formatted paper. This approach keeps the manuscript closer to its source data and makes collaboration more transparent and reproducible \citep{ram2013git, braga2023github, himmelstein2020open}. Because \LaTeX{} separates content from formatting, the same source can also be adapted to journal templates with less manual reformatting \citep{bar2021reproducible, krewinkel2017formatting}.

Here, we use this workflow in a taxonomic review of the Afrotropical dung beetle genus \textit{Amietina} Cambefort, 1981 (Coleoptera: Scarabaeinae: Onthophagini). The genus currently contains four described species from forests in West, Central, and eastern Africa \citep{cambefort1981amietina, branco1989deux}. Material collected in the Eastern Arc Mountains of Tanzania revealed four additional species, broadening the morphological limits of the genus. The distributions of these species also add to the limited knowledge of dung beetle endemism across the Eastern Arc mountain blocks \citep{burgess2007biological, montanaro2024microallopatric}. Herein, we describe \textit{A. bondarevi} \textbf{n. sp.}, \textit{A. grebennikovi} \textbf{n. sp.}, \textit{A. mazumbaiensis} \textbf{n. sp.}, and \textit{A. praedicta} \textbf{n. sp.} as well as the previously unknown female of \textit{A. nyassalandica}. Additonally, we provide new distributional records, and present an updated identification key. Alongside these taxonomic contributions, we show how Phenoscript, GitHub, and Overleaf can connect semantic phenotype data directly to a publication-ready manuscript.




\section*{Materials and Methods}



\subsection*{Workflow integrating Phenoscript, GitHub, and Overleaf}

We organized the project as a four-step workflow linking semantic description, version control, collaborative writing, and manuscript rendering (Fig. \ref{fig:workflow}). The GitHub repository contains the Phenoscript source files, generated outputs, manuscript source, bibliography, and figures, thereby keeping the material required to reproduce the taxonomic treatments in one version-controlled project.

\textbf{Step 1: Writing and processing semantic descriptions.}
We wrote the descriptions of the four new species as \texttt{.yphs} files in \href{https://code.visualstudio.com/}{Visual Studio Code}, using the dedicated \href{https://marketplace.visualstudio.com/items?itemName=Tarasov-Lab.phenoscript}{Phenoscript extension}. The Phenoscript workflow parsed each description into an ontology-based knowledge graph, checked it against the project validation rules, and generated machine-readable OWL and human-readable Markdown and HTML outputs \citep{montanaro2024beyond, montanaro2024computable}. Thus, the semantic source and the text used in the taxonomic treatment were produced from the same description.

\textbf{Step 2: Preparing the manuscript.}
We wrote the manuscript in \LaTeX{} using \href{https://www.overleaf.com/}{Overleaf}. Overleaf provided a shared editing environment and compiled the manuscript, bibliography, figures, cross-references, and imported descriptions into a formatted PDF. Because formatting was controlled by the \LaTeX{} document class and commands, changes to the journal style did not require manual reformatting of each taxonomic treatment.

\textbf{Step 3: Versioning and synchronizing the project.}
We used GitHub as the link between the local Phenoscript project and the Overleaf project. Changes made locally were committed and pushed to the GitHub repository, which preserved the revision history of both the semantic descriptions and their generated outputs. The repository was then synchronized with Overleaf through its GitHub integration. This arrangement allowed collaborators to compare revisions, recover earlier versions, and update the manuscript and its source data within the same project \citep{ram2013git, braga2023github}.

\textbf{Step 4: Rendering descriptions in the manuscript.}
We inserted each generated Markdown description directly into the corresponding taxonomic treatment with the \verb|\markdownInput{}| command from the \texttt{markdown} package. After a Phenoscript source file was revised, we regenerated its outputs, committed them to GitHub, and synchronized the repository with Overleaf. The next manuscript compilation then rendered the revised description without manual copying or Markdown-to-\LaTeX{} conversion. The Phenoscript file therefore remained the authoritative source for both the computable phenotype data and the description read in the manuscript.

\subsection*{Semantic descriptions using Phenoscript}

We prepared separate Phenoscript descriptions for \textit{Amietina bondarevi}, \textit{A. grebennikovi}, \textit{A. mazumbaiensis}, and \textit{A. praedicta}, following the approach of \citet{montanaro2024beyond} and \citet{montanaro2024computable}. To keep descriptions consistent among species, we used the same order of anatomical regions and followed the project \href{https://github.com/sergeitarasov/phenoscript-workshop-incol-2026/wiki/Annotation-Guide}{annotation guide}. Comments in the \texttt{.yphs} files record the corresponding conventional wording and make the semantic statements easier to review.

Each statement links an anatomical entity to a quality via relationship. Anatomical terms were drawn primarily from the Anatomy Ontology of Insect Skeleto-Muscular system (AISM) and the Coleoptera Anatomy Ontology (COLAO; \citealp{giron2024colao}). Phenotypic qualities were represented with terms from the Phenotype and Trait Ontology (PATO), spatial regions with the Biological Spatial Ontology (BSPO), relations with the Relations Ontology (RO), and measurement units with the Units of Measurement Ontology (UO). 

We processed each source description to produce an OWL representation of the phenotype and checked the output against the validation rules implemented in the Phenoscript workflow. The final Markdown files present the same statements in a readable form and retain hyperlinks from anatomical and phenotypic terms to their ontology records. These Markdown files, rather than separately written descriptions, are the files rendered in the four new-species treatments below.


\subsection*{Material examined and morphology}
According to the identification key by \cite{orbigny1913synopsis} on African Onthophagini, the new species could belong to the \textit{Onthophagus} species-groups 3 (females) or 19 (males; dichotomy 41(34)). The comparison with the original descriptions of all valid species of those groups did not result in any correspondence.

Both male and female specimens of all described \textit{Amietina} species were examined, except for \textit{A. nyassalandica} of which we studied a single female, while male characters were retrieved from the detailed original description by \cite{branco1989deux}. Although we did not examine type material of any of the species, we studied specimens of \textit{A. cambeforti}, \textit{A. eburnea} and \textit{A. larrochei} coming from the same collecting events of those examined by \cite{branco1989deux} and on whose correct identification we have no doubts. 
Studied specimens are preserved in the following collections:

MZHF--Finnish Museum of Natural History LUOMUS, Helsinki, Finland; 

GMOC--Giulio Montanaro personal collection, Creazzo, Italy;

JFJC--Jean-Fran\c{c}ois Josso personal collection, Muzillac, France;

An additional specimen from the Rubeho Mountains putatively attributable to \textit{Amietina sensu novo} was identified among the samples registered in the \href{https://boldsystems.org/}{Barcode of Life Database} (BOLD) (unique ID: \href{https://boldsystems.org/index.php/Public_RecordView?processid=VGH254-12}{CNCCOLVG00004064}) by the colleague Vasily Grebennikov (Canadian Food Inspection Agency, Ottawa, Canada). For male genitalia, the length of the phallobase was measured from the middle of the antero-dorsal bulge to the middle of the postero-dorsal margin; parameral length was measured from the postero-dorsal angle to the apex (\textit{i.e.}, the distalmost part) of the left paramere. Pictures of morphology were taken with a Canon EOS 5DS and those of genitalia with a Canon EOS 250D connected to a stereomicroscope. The distribution map was realised in QGIS \citep{QGIS_software} and Eastern Arc forests outlined using the Terrestrial Ecoregions of the World layer \citep{olson2001terrestrial}.

For holotype labelling, we use “//” to separate different labels and “/” to separate lines on the same label. Comments to labels are given between square brackets. The following abbreviations are used: wc (white cardboard), rc (red cardboard), hb (handwritten in black), pb (printed in black).

\textbf{Type species}: \textit{Amietina larrochei} Cambefort, 1981 (by original designation).

\textbf{Diagnosis}. The genus is a member of the tribe Onthophagini. It can be differentiated from all other genera and subgenera of \textit{Onthophagus} by the following combination of characters: 1) body small (2.7--4.1 mm), lenticular; 2) antennae with 8 segments; 3) clypeus with two teeth; 4) protibiae with 3 teeth on dorsal margin (Figs. \ref{fig:fig3}J---O); 5) male protibiae with a small, sharp uncus (Figs. \ref{fig:fig3}J,L,M,O); 6) dorsal body surface covered with simple punctures bearing conspicuous setae; 7) parameral apices acuminate and bent ventrally (Figs. \ref{fig:fig4}--\ref{fig:fig5}); 8) lamella copulatrix composed by two adjacent but separate endophallites, usually with an additional, elongated small distal endophallite (Figs. \ref{fig:fig5}E--I). 
The following two characters, previously used to define the genus, are here reconsidered: 1) head surface either devoid of protrusions (but sometimes with a transverse bulging on the frons) (Figs. \ref{fig:fig3}A--D) or with a small frontal tubercle or short carina (Figs. \ref{fig:fig3}E--I); 2) distal protibial margin from perpendicularly to obliquely truncated (Figs. \ref{fig:fig3}J--O).

\textbf{Biology}. Based on literature \citep{branco1989deux} and the present data, \textit{Amietina} species are forest-specialised. Specimens were collected in ant nest debris, excrements, rotten fish, fruits and dead diplopods, suggesting a rather generalist feeding behaviour (present data; \citealp{branco1989deux, cambefort1981amietina, moretto2010onthophagus}).

\textbf{Distribution}. The genus is endemic to the Afrotropical region.

\textbf{Remarks}. \textit{Amietina sensu novo} maintains a substantially similar definition to its previous concept \citep{branco1989deux, cambefort1981amietina}. The only differences are that the distal margin of protibiae can be either perpendicularly (as in the previous concept, except for \textit{A. larrochei}) or obliquely truncated, and that the head can be either unarmed (previous concept) or with protrusions (new concept). Furthermore, \textit{Amietina} species are characterised by a lamella copulatrix formed by 3 separate sclerites characteristically arranged (Figs. \ref{fig:fig5}E--I). The only exception is \textit{A. bondarevi}, which has only 2 evident sclerites, but this may be due to the poor sclerification of the endophallus of the only examined specimen, which may have rendered the third, smaller sclerite less clearly visible.

Based on morphology, the genus can be subdivided into three operational species-groups:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textit{Amietina larrochei} species-group, characterized by: 1) head without protrusions in both sexes, surface at most swollen medially (Figs. \ref{fig:fig3}A--D); 2) distal margin of male protibiae perpendicularly truncated or almost so (Figs. \ref{fig:fig3}J,L); 3) proximal region of endophallus without raspulae (Figs. \ref{fig:fig4}A,E); 4) pronotal punctuation from uniform to irregular, occasionally with some distinctly smaller punctures intermixed with bigger ones (\textit{A. larrochei}) or with micropunctuation (\textit{A. nyassalandica}) (Fig. \ref{fig:fig1}). The group comprises \textit{A. cambeforti} Branco, 1989, \textit{A. eburnea} Cambefort, 1981, \textit{A. larrochei} Cambefort, 1981 and, putatively, as we could not examine male genitalia, \textit{A. nyassalandica} Branco, 1989.
    \item \textit{Amietina mazumbaiensis} species-group, characterized by: 1) head with a tubercle between the anterior margin of the eyes (males) or a short, arched carina on the frons (females) (Figs. \ref{fig:fig3}E--H); 2) distal margin of male and female protibiae obliquely truncated (Figs. \ref{fig:fig3}M--N); 3) proximal region of endophallus with raspulae (Figs. \ref{fig:fig5}A--C); 4) pronotal punctuation clearly heterogeneous, composed of larger punctures intermixed with many smaller ones. The group comprises \textit{A. grebennikovi} \textbf{n. sp.}, \textit{A. mazumbaiensis} \textbf{n. sp.} and \textit{A. praedicta} \textbf{n. sp}.
    \item \textit{Amietina bondarevi} species-group, characterized by: 1) head with a short carina between the anterior margin of the eyes (Fig. \ref{fig:fig3}I); 2) distal edge of protibiae obliquely truncated (Fig. \ref{fig:fig3}O); 3) proximal region of endophallus without raspulae (Fig. \ref{fig:fig5}D); 4) pronotal punctuation clearly heterogeneous, composed of larger punctures intermixed with many smaller ones. The group comprises the only \textit{A. bondarevi} \textbf{n. sp}.
\end{enumerate}

Other undescribed species may also belong to the genus as defined here (\citealp{moretto2010onthophagus}; P. Moretto, personal communication) (see also the Discussion).

\section*{Discussion}


\subsection*{Semantic publishing workflow}

The need to publish taxonomic papers as structured text has long been recognized \citep{penev2010semantic, penev2011xml, miller2012cybertaxonomic}. In conventional documents, taxon names, treatments, specimen records, and figures are visible to readers but are not always identified in a way that computers can easily interpret. Structured markup gives these elements explicit roles, allowing them to be found, linked, extracted, and reused. TaxPub format introduced such markup for taxonomic literature, while Pensoft's ARPHA platform allows manuscripts to be written and reviewed online and published as structured XML and HTML  \citep{penev2011xml, stoev2018arpha}. Together with persistent identifiers, accessible repositories, and suitable metadata, these developments can make taxonomic publications more consistent with the FAIR principles \citep{wilkinson2016fair}.

Comparable systems have been developed outside taxonomy. Pandoc Scholar generates several publication formats from one plain-text source, whereas Manubot combines text-based writing, Git version control, and automated manuscript generation \citep{krewinkel2017formatting, himmelstein2020open}. Both follow the same principle: content should be recorded once in a structured source and reused to produce the required outputs.

Our workflow applies this principle to semantic species descriptions, an emerging approach in taxonomy \citep{balhoff2013semantic, deans2015phenotypes, montanaro2024computable}. Phenoscript generates computable phenotype data and readable text from the same source. GitHub records changes, and Overleaf imports the generated Markdown into the \LaTeX{} manuscript. A correction is therefore made in Phenoscript and propagated to the manuscript after regeneration and synchronization, rather than copied into several versions by hand. This  keeps the published treatment aligned with its semantic source, and preserves a shared revision history \citep{ram2013git, braga2023github}.


Wider adoption of these principles in taxonomy faces two main barriers. First, \LaTeX{} remains uncommon among taxonomists and is not accepted by many taxonomic journals. Yet its use can reduce manual reformatting and streamline production, potentially lowering publication costs \citep{bar2021reproducible, krewinkel2017formatting}. Broader support for \LaTeX{} among authors, journals, and publishers would therefore benefit taxonomy.

Second, Phenoscript requires authors to learn its syntax and the basic principles of ontologies. Wider use will depend on community-led training and engagement. Artificial-intelligence tools may help convert existing descriptions or prepare initial Phenoscript drafts, although their output will still require validation and expert review.

A smaller practical limitation is that some Overleaf features require a subscription, although institutional access may be available. Overleaf is convenient but not essential: authors can instead use a free local \LaTeX{} distribution and text editor together with GitHub. The essential requirement is a text-based, version-controlled manuscript into which generated Phenoscript outputs can be incorporated without duplication.


This study shows that semantic descriptions can be used throughout a complete taxonomic revision. Publication alone, however, does not make them readily available for comparative use. A centralized repository is badly needed where semantic phenotypes can be deposited, retrieved, queried, and compared across taxa and publications. Besides providing these services, such a repository could also provide a shared environment for engaging the taxonomic community in the adoption and development of new technologies.








\subsection*{Paragraph on Amietina to be added at the end}

The Eastern Arc Mountains in Tanzania and Kenya are one of the most important hotspots of biodiversity on the earth, hosting an exaggerated amount of endemic plant and animal species \citep{burgess2007biological}. However, dung beetle diversity in the EAMs is virtually unknown. The only available study on the subject reported an instance of microallopatric speciation within a single mountain block in the epactoidine genus \textit{Grebennikovius} Mlambo, Sole \& Scholtz, 2019 \citep{montanaro2024microallopatric}, but nothing is known about the patterns of endemism of other dung beetle lineages. The \textit{Amietina} species described here are likely endemic to the separate mountains where they were discovered. In particular, \textit{A. grebennikovi}, \textit{A. mazumbaiensis}, and \textit{A. praedicta} form a complex of morphologically very similar species that may have originated through allopatric speciation in the Udzungwa, West Usambara, and Uluguru Mountains, respectively, making them the first documented case of microendemism in which separate EAM mountains host different, strictly related dung beetle species. This suggests that additional \textit{Amietina} species occur in other EAM regions, including the Rubeho Mountains, where a putative \textit{Amietina} specimen was collected (see Methods) but could not be examined in the present study.


\section*{Conclusions}

Taxonomy must keep pace with the rapid development of digital biology. Although analytical methods have advanced considerably, species descriptions and manuscripts are still often prepared as conventional text, limiting efficient workflows, computational reuse, and integration with other biodiversity data.

This study demonstrates that semantic species descriptions can be incorporated into a complete taxonomic revision and carried directly from their computable source into a publication-ready manuscript. The integrated use of Phenoscript, GitHub, and Overleaf provides a practical model for producing taxonomic treatments that remain readable to taxonomists while being available for computational reuse.

The revision also reveals previously undocumented diversity in the Eastern Arc Mountains by adding four new species of \textit{Amietina}. Their restricted distributions provide further evidence that separate mountain blocks harbour distinct dung beetle faunas and emphasize the importance of documenting the remaining rainforest fragments. Together, these methodological and taxonomic results show how structured digital approaches can support both the study of biodiversity and the preservation of the knowledge derived from it.
