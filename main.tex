\begin{abstract}
Modern taxonomy increasingly relies on digital infrastructures to improve transparency, reproducibility, and interoperability, yet many available tools remain disconnected within the taxonomic workflow. Here, we present an integrated approach combining semantic morphological descriptions in Phenoscript, version-controlled data management through GitHub, and collaborative manuscript preparation in Overleaf. By linking structured phenotypic data, manuscript development, and FAIR data publication standards, this workflow facilitates reproducible taxonomic research and improves the accessibility and reusability of taxonomic knowledge.

We apply this workflow to a review of the dung beetle genus \textit{Amietina} Cambefort, 1981 (Coleoptera: Scarabaeidae: Onthophagini), which we redefine to accomodate four new species from the Eastern Arc Mountains (Tanzania): \textit{A. bondarevi} \textbf{n. sp.}, \textit{A. grebennikovi} \textbf{n. sp.}, \textit{A. mazumbaiensis} \textbf{n. sp.} and \textit{A. praedicta} \textbf{n. sp.}. Furthermore, we describe the previously unknown female of \textit{A. nyassalandica} Branco, 1989 and provide an updated identification key to the genus. The newly described species represent the first documented case of mountain-block endemism among Eastern Arc dung beetles, highlighting the hidden diversity and conservation importance of these threatened rainforest ecosystems.

Together, this study demonstrates how integrated digital workflows can enhance the efficiency, reproducibility, and interoperability of taxonomic research.

\textbf{Keywords:} FAIR principles, collaborative writing, LaTex, new species, Onthophagini, Eastern Arc Mountains, Tanzania

\end{abstract}

\section*{ST-Introduction}

Taxonomic studies depend on the accurate production, management, and publication of large quantities of descriptive information. Most taxonomic manuscripts are prepared using general-purpose word processors, particularly Microsoft Word (including its cloud-based version) or Google Docs.  These applications provide familiar writing environments and support comments, suggested edits, and simultaneous collaboration.
Nevertheless, they  have limitations for taxonomic projects which are often data-intensive (Ref to Arpha paper). Changes may be scattered across multiple document versions, making revisions difficult to track and earlier text hard to recover, particularly in collaborative projects. Substantial effort may also be required to format a word-processing document according to the requirements of a particular journal, which takes unncesseary time that taxonomist could deveote to scientific finding, instead for stylziing.

Another critical issues is that conventional taxonomic descriptions are written primarily as natural-language text. Although such descriptions communicate morphological information effectively, their contents are not directly interpretable by computers. This limits the automated extraction, integration,  and comparison of phenotypic information across publications and taxa as well as the reuse of this infomation in biodiversity  studies. Phenoscript addresses this limitation by using biological ontologies to encode traits semantically \citep{ADD_PHENOSCRIPT_REFERENCE}. Its descriptions can be rendered as both human-readable text for publication and machine-readable data for computational querying and integration. The advantage -- All terms in Phenoscript human-redable description are hyperlinked with cloud-based onotlgies that allow checking a defintion of for example an antomical terms right from within the descriontpm.

In a basic Phenoscript-assisted workflow, the generated natural-language description can be copied into a manuscript. This copy-paste retains an important source of duplication. Whenever the original Phenoscript description is corrected or expanded, the natural-language text must be generated again and recopied into the manuscript. Repeated copying along with the outlined issues of manuscript preparation --version control and stylizing -- may create errors, require extra time and and increases the risk of divergence between the underlying observations and the final publication. 

Here, we present an integrated workflow for streamlining semantic taxonomic manuscripts that reduces repetitive copying, provides transparent version control, and minimizes time spent on formatting. Phenoscript descriptions are written in \href{https://code.visualstudio.com/}{Visual Studio Code}, a freely available text editor supporting Phenoscript language. The complete taxonomic project is stored in \href{https://github.com/}{GitHub}, an online platform that records every change made to the files, allows collaborators to compare revisions and recover earlier versions, and can provide public access to all materials associated with the study. The manuscript is prepared in \href{https://www.overleaf.com/}{Overleaf}, a collaborative online platform for writing \LaTeX{} documents that supports simultaneous editing, automatic compilation, reference management, and journal-specific templates \citep{sanchez_egea_garcia_vilana_gonzalez_rojas_napoles_alberro_2026}. GitHub connects the Phenoscript files with Overleaf, allowing updated human-readable descriptions to be synchronized with the manuscript without repeated copying and pasting.  Together, these tools provide taxonomists with a reproducible workflow in which descriptions are written once, their revision history is preserved, and publication-ready text is generated and incorporated directly into the manuscript. By storing openly accessible files in GitHub and encoding phenotypic information with standardized ontology terms, the workflow makes taxonomic data more findable, accessible, interoperable, and reusable (FAIR).








The novelty of this workflow lies not simply in the individual use of Phenoscript, GitHub, or Overleaf, but in connecting them into a continuous path from morphological observation to publication. The semantic description becomes the authoritative source for both the human-readable taxonomic treatment and the computable phenotype data. Version control records how this source changes, while manuscript integration ensures that the published text remains aligned with it. The resulting ``write once, generate multiple outputs'' approach reduces repetitive work, improves consistency and transparency, and makes taxonomic descriptions reusable beyond the publication in which they originally appear.

We demonstrate this workflow through a taxonomic review of the Afrotropical dung beetle genus \textit{Amietina} Cambefort, 1981. Species descriptions were written in Phenoscript, managed and version-controlled through GitHub, and incorporated into an Overleaf manuscript as automatically generated human-readable treatments. In addition to revising the taxonomy of \textit{Amietina}, this study provides a practical example of how semantic technologies and established collaborative platforms can be integrated into the routine preparation of taxonomic publications.

\section*{Introduction}
%---
Taxonomic revisions depend on the accurate production, management, and publication of large amounts of descriptive information. Traditionally, morphological observations are first recorded in notes, spreadsheets, or specialized databases and are then manually rewritten or copied into species descriptions for publication. This separation between data collection and manuscript preparation creates duplicated work and increases the risk of inconsistencies, transcription errors, and divergence between the underlying observations and the final published text. Moreover, conventional taxonomic descriptions are written primarily for human readers, which limits their direct use in computational analyses, database integration, and large-scale comparisons across taxa.

%----------
In recent years, digital tools and data infrastructures have become increasingly important for modern taxonomy, enabling more transparent, reproducible, and interoperable research \citep{penev2017strategies, smith2013cybertaxonomy}. Semantic description languages, collaborative writing platforms, code repositories, and standardized biodiversity data formats address different aspects of the taxonomic workflow, but are often used independently. Integrating these components into a unified framework can streamline taxonomic practice while improving the accessibility of taxonomic knowledge.

We developed an integrated taxonomic workflow that combines semantic morphological descriptions written in Phenoscript, version-controlled data management through GitHub, and collaborative manuscript preparation in Overleaf. Phenoscript is a semantic language that encodes phenotypic information in both human- and machine-readable formats, enabling morphological descriptions to be interpreted by taxonomists while being computationally reusable \citep{montanaro2024beyond, montanaro2024computable}. Overleaf is a LaTex-based collaborative writing platform that supports real-time editing, version tracking, and the integration of structured data into scientific manuscripts \citep{sanchez_egea_garcia_vilana_gonzalez_rojas_napoles_alberro_2026}. GitHub serves as the central repository linking Phenoscript and Overleaf, providing version control, collaborative editing, and public access to all files associated with this publication. This integration ensures that updates to the source files are automatically reflected in the manuscript while minimizing transcription errors \citep{dabbish2012social}.

To further enhance interoperability, nomenclatural acts are published as nanopublications \citep{kuhn2017genuine, kuhn2013broadening, rossini2024towards}, machine-readable units of knowledge that can be readily discovered and reused on the Semantic Web, while distributional data are released in Darwin Core format for integration with biodiversity repositories \citep{wieczorek2012darwin}. Together, these approaches follow FAIR principles by improving the findability, accessibility, interoperability, and reusability of taxonomic data \citep{wilkinson2016fair}.

Here, we implement this workflow by reviewing the dung beetle genus \textit{Amietina} Cambefort, 1981 (Coleoptera: Scarabaeidae: Onthophagini). \textit{Amietina} belongs to the hyperdiverse tribe Onthophagini (\textit{sensu} \citealp{Montanaro2026.07.22.740134}) and is one of the few genera presenting only 8 antennomeres instead of the usual 9 \citep{branco1992essai, cambefort1981amietina, cambefort1979donnees, cambefort1986une, deschodt2023new}. The genus is well characterised by a unique combination of homoplastic traits: 1) the absence of a 4\textsuperscript{th} tooth on the protibial margin; 2) the distal protibial margin most often perpendicularly truncated, always so in males; 3) the presence of an uncus in the distoventral margin of male protibiae; 4) the bidentate clypeus; 5) the absence of any head protrusion except for a blunt, transverse gibbosity; and 6) the body small and lenticular \citep{branco1989deux, cambefort1981amietina}. \textit{Amietina} currently comprises four described species, two of them found in West and Central-West African forests (\textit{A. larrochei} Cambefort, 1981 and \textit{A. eburnea} Cambefort, 1981), one in Uganda (\textit{A. cambeforti} Branco, 1988) and one in Malawi (\textit{A. nyassalandica} Branco, 1988) \citep{branco1989deux, cambefort1981amietina}.

During an ongoing survey of the dung beetle fauna of the Tanzanian Eastern Arc Mountains biodiversity hotspot undertaken by GM, we gathered material of four very similar, undescribed species of Onthophagini from the rainforests of the Udzungwa, Uluguru and West Usambara Mountains. The taxa match \textit{Amietina} for most traits, yet they differ in having the protibiae always obliquely truncated and a carina or tubercle on the head. In which onthophagine (sub)genus should these species be included? To understand it, we studied the morphology of all described \textit{Amietina}, including phylogenetically informative characters of male genitalia that were never employed before. This leads us to expand and redefine the concept of the genus, in which we accommodate the four new taxa from Tanzania---\textit{Amietina praedicta} n. sp., \textit{Amietina grebennikovi} n. sp., \textit{Amietina bondarevi} n. sp. and \textit{Amietina mazumbaiensis} n. sp. Moreover, we describe for the first time the female of \textit{A. nyassalandica}, provide new distributional records and an updated identification key to the genus. Lastly, we stress how the new taxa represent the first documented case of dung beetle microendemism in the Eastern Arc mountains in which closely allied species diversified allopatrically in different mountains.


\section*{Materials and Methods}

In practice, a taxonomist writes or edits a semantic description in Phenoscript using the freely available \href{https://code.visualstudio.com/}{Visual Studio Code} editor and the Phenoscript extension \citep{ADD_PHENOSCRIPT_REFERENCE}. The description is then converted into machine-readable semantic data and a human-readable Markdown representation. These outputs are committed to GitHub and synchronized with the corresponding Overleaf project. The Markdown description is rendered within the \LaTeX{} manuscript, so that the taxonomic treatment displayed in Overleaf is generated directly from the semantic source. Thus, a modification made to a Phenoscript description can be propagated to the manuscript through a small number of synchronization commands, without duplicating the descriptive text.

\subsection*{Integration of Phenoscript, Github and Overleaf}

The preparation of this manuscript was done by integrating Phenoscript and Overleaf by linking them through GitHub using the following workflow (Fig. \ref{}):

\begin{enumerate}
\item Taxonomic descriptions are written with \href{https://github.com/sergeitarasov/PhenoScript}{PhenoScript}, which uses terms from anatomy ontologies to build computer-parsable taxonomic descriptions. We follow the general workflow developed in previous papers \citep{montanaro2024beyond, montanaro2024computable}, with only slight modifications in syntax and terminology. A complete annotation guide to write descriptions in the most consistent way can be found on \href{https://github.com/sergeitarasov/phenoscript-workshop-incol-2026/wiki/Annotation-Guide}{GitHub}. Files generated with Phenoscript include the yphs original code and its natural language-like translation in Markdown, consisting of human-readable text with hyperlinks to all anatomical terms used.

\item Manuscript preparation is performed in Overleaf, a cloud-based collaborative platform that uses the LaTeX document preparation system. LaTeX is a markup language widely used in scientific publishing that enables structured document creation and automated formatting of references, figures, tables, and other elements. Overleaf facilitates collaborative writing, version tracking, and the automatic rendering of the manuscript into a formatted PDF version, which can be easily used for submission and preprint publication.

\item Phenoscript files are uploaded to a GitHub repository which is then synchronised with the Overleaf project (Integrations > GitHub: Sync with a GitHub repository). Synchronisation allows to upload on the repository all files involved in the project, including the Tex file with the main manuscript text, figures, and references. The GitHub repository is managed via GitHub Desktop, an application that enables users to create a local copy of the repository on their computers, where files can be edited, version-controlled, and synchronized with the online repository through commits and pushes.

\item Once the Overleaf project is synchronised with GitHub, the natural language-like descriptions in Markdown were automatically incorporated and rendered within the LaTeX draft of the manuscript using the \texttt{\textbackslash markdownInput\{\}} function. This integration ensures that descriptions are automatically updated in the manuscript whenever the corresponding files are modified in GitHub, reducing the risk of transcription errors and avoiding manual copy-pasting or formatting issues associated with Markdown-to-LaTeX conversion.

\end{enumerate}


Moreover, the nomenclatural acts establishing the new species names were published, in addition to the regular Zoobank registration, in the form of nanopublications. Nanopubs were realised in \href{https://nanodash.petapico.org/publish?}{nanodash} using the \href{https://nanodash.petapico.org/publish?11&template=https://w3id.org/np/RAa7sIgQ77jJL4HYYGVH8sNu1YEKCH3F-CHqHrgbWWGS4}{format for defining new taxa} already tested by \cite{montanaro2024beyond}. Collecting data of all examined specimens were formatted in DarwinCore and are available in the supplementary material.

\textbf{Type species}: \textit{Amietina larrochei} Cambefort, 1981 (by original designation).

\textbf{Diagnosis}. The genus is a member of the tribe Onthophagini. It can be differentiated from all other genera and subgenera of \textit{Onthophagus} by the following combination of characters: 1) body small (2.7--4.1 mm), lenticular; 2) antennae with 8 segments; 3) clypeus with two teeth; 4) protibiae with 3 teeth on dorsal margin (Figs. \ref{fig:fig3}J---O); 5) male protibiae with a small, sharp uncus (Figs. \ref{fig:fig3}J,L,M,O); 6) dorsal body surface covered with simple punctures bearing conspicuous setae; 7) parameral apices acuminate and bent ventrally (Figs. \ref{fig:fig4}--\ref{fig:fig5}); 8) lamella copulatrix composed by two adjacent but separate endophallites, usually with an additional, elongated small distal endophallite (Figs. \ref{fig:fig5}E--I). 
The following two characters, previously used to define the genus, are here reconsidered: 1) head surface either devoid of protrusions (but sometimes with a transverse bulging on the frons) (Figs. \ref{fig:fig3}A--D) or with a small frontal tubercle or short carina (Figs. \ref{fig:fig3}E--I); 2) distal protibial margin from perpendicularly to obliquely truncated (Figs. \ref{fig:fig3}J--O).

\textbf{Biology}. Based on literature \citep{branco1989deux} and the present data, \textit{Amietina} species are forest-specialised. Specimens were collected in ant nest debris, excrements, rotten fish, fruits and dead diplopods, suggesting a rather generalist feeding behaviour (present data; \citealp{branco1989deux, cambefort1981amietina, moretto2010onthophagus}).

\textbf{Distribution}. The genus is endemic to the Afrotropical region.

\textbf{Remarks}. \textit{Amietina sensu novo} maintains a substantially similar definition to its previous concept \citep{branco1989deux, cambefort1981amietina}. The only differences are that the distal margin of protibiae can be either perpendicularly (as in the previous concept, except for \textit{A. larrochei}) or obliquely truncated, and that the head can be either unarmed (previous concept) or with protrusions (new concept). Furthermore, \textit{Amietina} species are characterised by a lamella copulatrix formed by 3 separate sclerites characteristically arranged (Figs. \ref{fig:fig5}E--I). The only exception is \textit{A. bondarevi}, which has only 2 evident sclerites, but this may be due to the poor sclerification of the endophallus, which may have rendered the third, smaller sclerite less clearly visible in the only examined specimen.

Based on morphology, the genus can be subdivided into three operational species-groups:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textit{Amietina larrochei} species-group, characterized by: 1) head without protrusions in both sexes, surface at most swollen medially (Figs. \ref{fig:fig3}A--D); 2) distal margin of male protibiae perpendicularly truncated or almost so (Figs. \ref{fig:fig3}J,L); 3) proximal region of endophallus without raspulae (Figs. \ref{fig:fig4}A,E); 4) pronotal punctuation from uniform to irregular, occasionally with some distinctly smaller punctures intermixed with bigger ones (\textit{A. larrochei}) or with micropunctuation (\textit{A. nyassalandica}) (Fig. \ref{fig:fig1}). The group comprises \textit{A. cambeforti} Branco, 1989, \textit{A. eburnea} Cambefort, 1981, \textit{A. larrochei} Cambefort, 1981 and, putatively, as we could not examine male genitalia, \textit{A. nyassalandica} Branco, 1989.
    \item \textit{Amietina mazumbaiensis} species-group, characterized by: 1) head with a tubercle between the anterior margin of the eyes (males) or a short, arched carina on the frons (females) (Figs. \ref{fig:fig3}E--H); 2) distal margin of male and female protibiae obliquely truncated (Figs. \ref{fig:fig3}M--N); 3) proximal region of endophallus with raspulae (Figs. \ref{fig:fig5}A--C); 4) pronotal punctuation clearly heterogeneous, composed of larger punctures intermixed with many smaller ones. The group comprises \textit{A. praedicta} \textbf{n. sp.}, \textit{A. grebennikovi} \textbf{n. sp.}, and \textit{A. mazumbaiensis} \textbf{n. sp}.
    \item \textit{Amietina bondarevi} species-group, characterized by: 1) head with a short carina between the anterior margin of the eyes (Fig. \ref{fig:fig3}I); 2) distal edge of protibiae obliquely truncated (Fig. \ref{fig:fig3}O); 3) proximal region of endophallus without raspulae (Fig. \ref{fig:fig5}D); 4) pronotal punctuation clearly heterogeneous, composed of larger punctures intermixed with many smaller ones. The group comprises the only \textit{A. bondarevi} \textbf{n. sp}.
\end{enumerate}

Other undescribed species may also belong to the genus as defined here (\citealp{moretto2010onthophagus}; P. Moretto, personal communication) (see also the Discussion).

\section*{Discussion}

\subsection{ST-some points}

Taxonomic studies depend on the accurate production, management, and publication of large quantities of descriptive information. In most taxonomic projects, manuscripts are prepared using general-purpose word processors, particularly Microsoft Word or Google Docs. These applications provide familiar writing environments and support comments, suggested edits, and simultaneous collaboration. Google Docs is particularly convenient for real-time editing, whereas Microsoft Word remains widely used by authors, journals, and publishers. Nevertheless, both approaches have limitations for complex and data-intensive taxonomic projects. Changes may be distributed among multiple document versions, comparisons among revisions can become difficult, and the provenance of individual modifications may not always be readily traceable. These problems become especially important in collaborative projects, in which descriptions are repeatedly revised and earlier formulations may need to be recovered.

Substantial effort may also be required to format a word-processing document according to the requirements of a particular journal. Although templates and predefined styles can reduce this burden, formatting may remain partly manual and may be disrupted when text, figures, tables, or references are moved between documents. More importantly, the manuscript usually remains separated from the underlying data from which its taxonomic treatments were produced. Consequently, corrections made to the source data are not necessarily propagated to the manuscript, and corrections made directly in the manuscript may not be reflected in the original dataset.

This separation is particularly consequential for species descriptions. Conventional taxonomic descriptions are written primarily as natural-language text intended for human readers. Although such descriptions communicate morphological information effectively, their contents are not directly interpretable by computers. This limits the automated extraction, integration, querying, and comparison of phenotypic information across publications and taxa. The same morphological observations may therefore need to be manually reinterpreted and recoded whenever they are reused in comparative, phylogenetic, or biodiversity-informatics studies.

Phenoscript addresses this limitation by providing a formal semantic language for describing organisms and their phenotypic traits \citep{ADD_PHENOSCRIPT_REFERENCE}. Phenoscript uses standardized concepts from biological ontologies to represent anatomical entities, phenotypic qualities, and the relationships among them. A description written in Phenoscript can consequently be transformed into both a human-readable natural-language description and a machine-readable semantic representation. The human-readable output can be included in a conventional taxonomic treatment, whereas the semantic output makes the same observations available for computational querying, integration, and comparative analysis.

In a basic Phenoscript-assisted workflow, the generated natural-language description can be copied into a manuscript. Although this already reduces the effort required to compose descriptions manually, it retains an important source of duplication. Whenever the original semantic description is corrected or expanded, the natural-language text must be generated again and recopied into the manuscript. Repeated copying and pasting is inconvenient and creates opportunities for outdated descriptions, transcription errors, or discrepancies between the semantic source and the published treatment.

More generally, maintaining morphological observations, semantic descriptions, generated natural-language text, and manuscript files as separate products fragments the research workflow. This fragmentation duplicates work and increases the risk of divergence between the underlying observations and the final publication. An improved workflow should instead maintain a single authoritative source for each description and propagate changes from that source directly into the manuscript.

Here, we present an integrated workflow that connects semantic species description, version-controlled data management, and collaborative manuscript preparation. The workflow combines descriptions written in Phenoscript, version control and repository management through GitHub, and manuscript preparation in Overleaf. Within this framework, Phenoscript descriptions are simultaneously human- and machine-readable products: they reuse standardized morphological terms from biological ontologies, can be computationally processed, and can be rendered as natural-language text suitable for publication.

\href{https://github.com/}{GitHub} is a platform for hosting and collaboratively managing files through the Git version-control system. Unlike ordinary document storage, Git records successive states of each tracked file, making it possible to identify changes, compare versions, attribute contributions, and recover earlier content. Proposed modifications can also be discussed and evaluated before they are incorporated into the main version of a project. Applied to semantic taxonomy, these functions provide a transparent history of how species descriptions were developed and revised. A public GitHub repository can additionally provide open access to the Phenoscript source files, generated descriptions, processing scripts, and other materials associated with a publication.

\href{https://www.overleaf.com/}{Overleaf} is a collaborative, \LaTeX-based writing platform that supports simultaneous editing, comments, document-history functions, automatic compilation, and the use of journal-specific templates \citep{sanchez_egea_garcia_vilana_gonzalez_rojas_napoles_alberro_2026}. In contrast to manual visual formatting in a word processor, a \LaTeX{} document separates its scientific content from many aspects of its presentation. Section headings, citations, cross-references, figures, tables, and other elements are formatted through explicit commands and document styles. A manuscript can therefore be adapted to a journal template without manually restyling each individual element. This is especially advantageous for lengthy taxonomic treatments containing numerous repeated and structurally similar species accounts.

GitHub provides the connection between Phenoscript and Overleaf. Overleaf supports synchronization with a linked GitHub repository, allowing changes to be transferred between the repository and the manuscript project. The Phenoscript source descriptions and their generated natural-language versions can therefore be maintained within the version-controlled repository, while the generated files are incorporated directly into the \LaTeX{} manuscript. When a description is modified, its publication-ready version can be regenerated, transferred through GitHub synchronization, and updated in Overleaf without manually copying and pasting the text.

In practice, a taxonomist writes or edits a semantic description in Phenoscript using the freely available \href{https://code.visualstudio.com/}{Visual Studio Code} editor and the Phenoscript extension \citep{ADD_PHENOSCRIPT_REFERENCE}. The description is then converted into machine-readable semantic data and a human-readable Markdown representation. These outputs are committed to GitHub and synchronized with the corresponding Overleaf project. The Markdown description is rendered within the \LaTeX{} manuscript, so that the taxonomic treatment displayed in Overleaf is generated directly from the semantic source. Thus, a modification made to a Phenoscript description can be propagated to the manuscript through a small number of synchronization commands, without duplicating the descriptive text.

The novelty of this workflow lies not simply in the individual use of Phenoscript, GitHub, or Overleaf, but in connecting them into a continuous path from morphological observation to publication. The semantic description becomes the authoritative source for both the human-readable taxonomic treatment and the computable phenotype data. Version control records how this source changes, while manuscript integration ensures that the published text remains aligned with it. The resulting ``write once, generate multiple outputs'' approach reduces repetitive work, improves consistency and transparency, and makes taxonomic descriptions reusable beyond the publication in which they originally appear.

We demonstrate this workflow through a taxonomic review of the Afrotropical dung beetle genus \textit{Amietina} Cambefort, 1981. Species descriptions were written in Phenoscript, managed and version-controlled through GitHub, and incorporated into an Overleaf manuscript as automatically generated human-readable treatments. In addition to revising the taxonomy of \textit{Amietina}, this study provides a practical example of how semantic technologies and established collaborative platforms can be integrated into the routine preparation of taxonomic publications.


\subsection*{Integration of Phenoscript, Github and Overleaf}

We implemented a workflow that streamlines taxonomic manuscript preparation by integrating Phenoscript, GitHub, and Overleaf into a unified digital environment. This approach enables the direct integration of semantic morphological descriptions into LaTeX-based manuscripts, reducing manual copy-pasting and formatting errors while improving consistency, reproducibility, and collaboration throughout the manuscript development process.

The broader application of this workflow is currently limited by the relatively low adoption of LaTeX-based submission systems among scientific (especially taxonomic) journals, many of which still rely on traditional text-editing environments \citep{bar2021reproducible}. Nevertheless, an increasing number of publishers have integrated Overleaf into their submission workflows, enabling authors to prepare and submit manuscripts within a unified collaborative environment that streamlines manuscript preparation and processing. Therefore, we hope that our approach will further encourage the adoption of more integrated publishing workflows as enabled by Overleaf, facilitating the incorporation of structured data and improving the efficiency of taxonomic communication.

A further objective of our workflow is to enhance the integration of computer-parsable taxonomic descriptions generated in Phenoscript into scientific publications. Phenoscript represents a promising tool for advancing phenomic applications by making phenotypic data more accessible to computational analyses. However, producing a Phenoscript description currently requires more effort than writing a traditional natural-language description. This limitation could be substantially reduced through the application of Artificial Intelligence models trained to convert existing natural-language descriptions into Phenoscript format (see an example here: \href{https://chatgpt.com/g/g-69d8e94a33e88191ac4dc13189a27606-phenoscript-gpt}{Phenoscript-GPT}). Such approaches could facilitate the conversion of both previously published and newly generated descriptions, allowing a broader community of taxonomists---including those with limited programming expertise---to benefit from the advantages of semantic, machine-interpretable descriptions.


\subsection*{\textit{Amietina} microendemism in the Eastern Arc Mountains} 
Until now, the genus \textit{Amietina} was known exclusively from western and central African forests \citep{davis2008african}. However, the discovery of additional species in eastern Africa is not entirely unexpected. The Eastern Arc Mountains (EAMs) are well-known refugia for rainforest species, many of which show their closest affinities with taxa from the Guineo-Congolian region (GCR) \citep{lovett2008biogeography, grebennikov2019miocene, grebennikov2021sky}. Furthermore, the dung beetle fauna of the EAMs seems to share some biogeographic affinities with that of the South East African Montane Archipelago (SEAMA) \citep{bayliss2024biogeographical}. Examples include the genus \textit{Janssensantus} Paulian, 1976 (Montanaro \textit{et al}., in preparation), \textit{Proagoderus mabuensis} Daniel, Josso, Nganhane \& Strümpher, 2024 and \textit{P. hemicyanus} (d'Orbigny, 1902) (\citealt{daniel2024dung}; GM, personal observation), as well as \textit{Onthophagus mabuensis} Daniel, Strümpher \& Josso, 2023 and several undescribed \textit{Onthophagus} from the EAMs (\citealt{daniel2023dung}; GM, unpublished data). These affinites suggest that additional \textit{Amietina} species may also inhabit the SEAMA.

The Eastern Arc Mountains in Tanzania and Kenya are one of the most important hotspots of biodiversity on the earth, hosting an exaggerated amount of endemic plant and animal species \citep{burgess2007biological}.
A common pattern of endemism in the EAM fauna is the occurrence of clades whose species evolved allopatrically on separate mountain blocks \citep{lovett2008biogeography, menegon2022cryptic, grebennikov2019miocene, grebennikov2017phylogeography, grebennikov2018survey}. However, no such cases have yet been documented in EAM dung beetles, whose diversity remains very poorly known. The only available study on the subject reported an instance of microallopatric speciation within a single mountain block in the epactoidine genus \textit{Grebennikovius} Mlambo, Sole \& Scholtz, 2019 \citep{montanaro2024microallopatric}.
However, it is likely that the \textit{Amietina} species described here are endemic to the separate mountains where they were discovered. In particular, \textit{A. grebennikovi}, \textit{A. mazumbaiensis}, and \textit{A. praedicta} form a complex of morphologically very similar species that may have originated through allopatric speciation in the Udzungwa, West Usambara, and Uluguru Mountains, respectively. Therefore, they probably represent the first documented case of ``single-mountain" endemism in EAM scarabaeines. This suggests that additional \textit{Amietina} species occur in other EAM regions, including the Rubeho Mountains, where a putative \textit{Amietina} specimen was collected (see Methods) but could not be examined in the present study. In fact, this biogeographic pattern is likely widespread among EAM dung beetles, as indicated by the existence of several species complexes within different lineages of Onthophagini (GM, unpublished data) and other \textit{incertae sedis} genera, such as \textit{Janssensantus} and \textit{Tanzanolus} (Montanaro \textit{et al}., in preparation), represented by distinct species on different EAM massifs.


\section*{Conclusions}

In this study, we implemented a publication workflow that integrates semantic taxonomic descriptions written in Phenoscript with collaborative manuscript preparation in the LaTeX-based Overleaf environment, linked through GitHub. We further enhanced the computational reusability of our data by publishing nomenclatural information as nanopublications and by formatting distributional data in Darwin Core format. Overall, this workflow streamlined the taxonomic review of the dung beetle genus \textit{Amietina} by facilitating collaboration, reducing manual data handling, and ensuring consistency between source data and the manuscript, while providing a practical framework for FAIR taxonomic publishing.

Secondly, this study represents a further contribution on the hidden dung beetle diversity of the Eastern Arc Mountains, adding four new \textit{Amietina} species to the known EAM fauna. Documenting this biodiversity is essential for revealing patterns of distribution and endemism in dung beetles, which are in turn crucial for guiding conservation efforts aimed at protecting the fragile rainforest patches that support these unique species.

Together, our results demonstrate how the integration of digital infrastructures with traditional taxonomic approaches can improve the reproducibility and accessibility of biodiversity research while accelerating the documentation of diversity in threatened ecosystems.
